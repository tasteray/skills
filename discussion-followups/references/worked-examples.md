# Worked Examples

Full before/after responses across all four exit types. Each example shows the *current* end-state of the response (long analysis ending in a conversational thread) and the *new* end-state (analysis → bridge → typed exits).

The point of these examples is to illustrate **the shape change**, not to prescribe verbatim wording. The bridge sentence and exit pivots must be generated fresh from the actual analysis above them.

---

## Example 1 — Type A (Scene / Song / Moment)

### The conversation so far

The user asked: *"give me deeper info on the 'sweet child of mine' performance at this movie"* about *Captain Fantastic* (2016).

### The analysis (unchanged)

> That "Sweet Child O' Mine" scene… it's doing way more heavy lifting than it looks.
>
> First — Matt Ross flips the song on purpose. The original Guns N' Roses version is chaotic, electric, a bit reckless. Here it's stripped down, almost folk-like. No distortion, no swagger. Just voices. That shift mirrors Ben's whole worldview: take something from mainstream culture and "purify" it into something intentional, almost ritualistic.
>
> But here's the deeper layer: they're singing it at the mother's funeral.
>
> So the lyrics land differently:
> - "Where do we go now?" stops being rock angst → it becomes literal grief
> - "Sweet child o' mine" isn't romantic anymore → it's about parenthood, legacy, loss
>
> And the key tension of the film shows up right there: Ben built this hyper-controlled, ideological bubble… but grief doesn't care about ideology. It cracks the system.
>
> What hits hardest (and I think this is why it stays with people like you): it's one of the few moments where the family isn't performing brilliance or discipline — they're just fragile together. No philosophy. No survival training. Just a family trying not to fall apart.

### BEFORE (current behavior)

> If you want, I can break down why that scene echoes the final "bus departure" moment — they're quietly rhyming with each other.

### AFTER (with this skill)

---

**That funeral scene works on you because it does the inverse of what the original song does — it strips swagger to expose grief. A few places to go.**

**Watch it deeper**
- The scene itself (official Captain Fantastic clip on YouTube) — worth a second pass now that you have the emotional thesis
- *Like Stories of Old: The Quiet Tragedy of Captain Fantastic* — picks up the ideology-vs-grief reading further than we did

**If this scene stayed with you**
- *Manchester by the Sea* (2016) — same engineering: a family using ritual to hold grief that no system can touch
- *A Late Quartet* (2012) — what happens when controlled artistry collides with mortality you can't rehearse for

Want to keep pulling on the bus-departure thread instead?

---

### What changed

- The original closing question is preserved, but **subordinated** — it's a quiet single line at the end, not a competing CTA.
- Three exits: a clip (visual companion), a video essay (visual deeper), two sibling films. Total: 4 exits. The continuation question is the 5th element but at clearly lower visual weight.
- Each sibling pivots on a specific phrase from the analysis: "family using ritual", "controlled artistry collides with mortality".
- No invented URLs — the sources are named with channel/source so they're verifiable.

### The conversation so far

The user asked: *"Why does Lynne Ramsay shoot *You Were Never Really Here* so fragmented? It feels like she's editing my memory, not the story."*

### The analysis (sketch)

[Long analysis of Ramsay's fragmented editing as a signature, her use of memory-logic rather than story-logic, the way she's developed this across her films, etc.]

### BEFORE (current behavior)

> Want me to compare it to how Scorsese uses similar techniques in *Bringing Out the Dead*?

### AFTER (with this skill)

---

**That fragmentation isn't a stylistic choice for this film — it's Ramsay's whole signature. She edits like memory, not like story, and you can watch her sharpening it across three films.**

**To see the technique evolve**
- *Morvern Callar* (2002) — where she first developed the fragmented-consciousness lexicon
- *We Need to Talk About Kevin* (2011) — same approach applied to motherhood instead of violence; the technique gets more precise

**To hear it in her own words**
- Lynne Ramsay interview on the *Director's Cut* podcast (DGA) — she talks specifically about editing for emotional logic over narrative logic

**For a useful contrast**
- *Bringing Out the Dead* (1999, Scorsese) — same fragmented-consciousness approach from a totally different sensibility; sharpens what's distinctive about Ramsay

---

### What changed

- Type C default shape: 1–2 director's films + 1 interview + 1 sibling film by different director.
- Note the **continuation question is gone entirely** — the exits cover all the directions the user might have wanted. One CTA, not both.
- The sibling-by-different-director exit (*Bringing Out the Dead*) is the original continuation thread, now repositioned as an exit with a clearer "why".
- All exits pivot on the **memory-logic vs. story-logic** thread.

## Cross-Cutting Observations

Reading these four examples side by side:

### The bridge sentence does heavy lifting

In every example, the bridge sentence accomplishes the same two moves:
1. **Names the emotional/intellectual payload** of the analysis in compressed form
2. **Announces the move** from analysis to next-steps

The bridge is often the most-rewritten part of the response. Worth taking the time.

### Section labels can adapt

The skill template uses "Watch it deeper" / "If this stayed with you" as defaults. But in worked examples:
- Type C uses "To see the technique evolve" / "To hear it in her own words" / "For a useful contrast"
- Type D uses "Closer to X than Y is" / "Opposite philosophy, similar tools" / "Maps it for you"

The skill enforces *visual hierarchy* (bolded section labels separating exit types). It does not enforce specific label text.

### Continuation question lives or dies based on whether exits cover it

If the exits already serve the natural next direction (Type C, D examples above), drop the continuation question entirely. If the exits go one direction but there's a clearly hotter thread the user might want (Type A, B examples above), keep the continuation as a quiet final line.

Never do both at full strength.

### No URLs in these examples

These worked examples don't include literal URLs. In production, with the tools available, exits should include actual links. Without tools, exits should name the source clearly (channel, podcast, official source) so the user can find it themselves — and *omit anything that can't be verified*.

## How to Use These Examples

When generating an exits block for a new question:

1. **Identify the type** (A/B/C/D) based on what the question is really asking
2. **Find the closest worked example above** as a structural template
3. **Generate fresh** the bridge sentence and pivots from the actual analysis
4. **Self-check** against the quality rules

Do not copy the wording. The pivots are the whole point, and they have to come from the specific conversation in front of you.
